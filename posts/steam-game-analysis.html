Are Expensive Steam Games Actually Better?

We expect to get what we pay for. But when it comes to PC games, do higher prices actually come with better player reviews?

01 / The question

Does a bigger price tag mean a better game?

Gaming can be expensive. A new release might cost more than $60, while another game is free or costs less than $10. It is tempting to assume that more expensive games offer a better experience. I wanted to test that assumption using something players leave behind in enormous quantities: Steam reviews.

Rather than trying to define game quality objectively, I measured player reception as the percentage of recorded reviews marked positive. I then compared that percentage with listed game prices, genres, community tags, publishers, and release years.

02 / Data acquisition

Building the dataset

I wrote a Python collection script that queried the Steam Spy API for popular Steam applications and then requested detailed information about individual games. The initial collection contained 4,996 records, including game names, prices, review counts, genres, and community tags. Requests were rate-limited, retried when appropriate, and cached as JSON so I would not repeatedly ask the API for the same information.

I added release dates from The Game Census, using a downloadable index rather than thousands of individual requests. The two sources were joined on Steam App ID. After removing duplicates, excluding incomplete price or review data, and requiring at least 50 reviews, 4,855 games remained; 4,358 had usable release dates.

From raw fields to features

Prices were converted from cents into US dollars. For each game, I calculated positive / (positive + negative) × 100 to obtain a positive-review percentage.

Handling many-to-many data

Genres, tags, and publishers were expanded into separate game-level relationship tables. This preserved multi-genre games without counting each game more than once in the core dataset.

Price categories use the API's original listed price, not necessarily what reviewers paid. Games with a recorded original price of zero are labeled “Free” in the plots.

03 / Finding one

The middle of the price range stands out

At first glance, there is no simple “more expensive means better” pattern. Among 3,779 paid games, Pearson's correlation between original listed price and positive-review percentage was just 0.021—essentially no linear association in this sample.

Grouping games into price categories reveals more detail. Games priced from $10.01 to $20 had the highest median positive-review percentage, 88.54%. Games over $40 had a lower median of 82.99%, while free games had a median of 78.22%.

Key finding

The best-reviewed price bracket in this dataset was not the most expensive one. But the pattern is descriptive, not proof that charging $10–$20 makes a game better.

04 / Finding two

Genre tells another part of the story

Price is only one way games differ. The genre boxplots show noticeably different review distributions: Indie, Casual, and Adventure games have relatively high medians, while Free To Play and Massively Multiplayer games have lower medians in this sample. This does not mean every game in a genre follows the same pattern; there is considerable variation within each group.

The next question is whether the mid-price advantage is specific to certain genres. The heatmap compares median ratings within genre–price combinations, excluding groups with fewer than 30 games so that very small groups do not dominate the interpretation.

The $10–$20 group performs well across Action, Adventure, Indie, RPG, and Strategy. Simulation is an exception: its $5.01–$10 group has a slightly higher median. These differences suggest that a single price comparison can conceal meaningful variation by game type.

05 / Finding three

Release year adds context

Games released in different years also show different review patterns. Median positive-review percentages dip for releases around 2014–2017, then rise for several later cohorts. For example, the 2015 cohort has a median of 82.03%, compared with 87.18% for games released in 2020.

That distinction matters. This figure compares groups of games released in different years; it cannot tell us whether Steam players became happier or less happy over time.

06 / Interpretation & ethics

What these results cannot tell us

There are several reasons to be cautious. Steam Spy's popular-game listings are not a random sample of everything on Steam. Review percentages reflect the people who choose to leave reviews, not everyone who plays a game. Original listed prices are not necessarily transaction prices, and genre and tag categories overlap. Release dates were missing for about 10% of the cleaned sample.

The conclusion also depends on filtering decisions. When I increased the minimum review threshold from 50 to 2,000, the price–rating correlation among paid games shifted from 0.021 to −0.196. That sensitivity is itself a useful finding: which games enter the analysis changes the apparent relationship.

For responsible data collection, I cached API responses, respected request pacing, and kept acquisition separate from the offline analysis. I did not publish raw collected datasets in the code repository; the scripts document how to reconstruct them from their sources. The Game Census data is licensed under CC BY 4.0, with attribution to The Game Census.

Bottom line

More expensive Steam games are not consistently better reviewed. Mid-priced games look strong in this sample, but genre, release cohort, and sampling choices all complicate the picture. Price alone is a poor shortcut for player reception.
